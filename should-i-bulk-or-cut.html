Why the mirror is the wrong input

Almost everyone asking this question is trying to answer it by looking at themselves — and that's exactly why they're still asking. The mirror moves with lighting, pump, time of day and mood. It tells you how you feel about your body, which has very little to do with what your body should be doing next.

The decision only needs one input: roughly how much fat you're carrying right now. Get that number even approximately right and the answer falls out on its own. Get it wrong and you'll spend months going the wrong direction — the classic version being a lean-ish person cutting again because they don't feel lean enough, when they actually needed more muscle all along.

The thresholds

These are general reference bands for men. Shift them up by roughly 8–10 points for women, since healthy body fat is naturally higher.

under 12%Bulk — you're lean enough that added calories go disproportionately toward muscle. Staying here indefinitely mostly costs you progress.
12–15%Bulk — the most productive place to start a gaining phase. Enough room to grow before fat becomes the limiting factor.
15–20%Either — a genuine judgement call. Cut if you want to see definition sooner; bulk if you're more interested in size and can accept looking softer for a while.
20–25%Cut — bulking from here adds fat faster than muscle and gets uncomfortable quickly. Bring it down first.
over 25%Cut — clearly. If you're also untrained, see the recomp section below — you may not have to choose at all.
These bands are widely used reference points, not precise cutoffs, and individuals vary. Frame, training history, and where you personally store fat all move the picture. Treat them as a starting direction rather than a rule.

What each one actually costs you

Bulking

You will get softer

Some fat gain is unavoidable — the goal is to keep it in proportion, not to avoid it. Expect to look worse before you look better, and expect that to be uncomfortable if you've just finished a cut.

What you get for it: muscle is gained slowly, and a surplus is the condition under which it's gained fastest.

Cutting

You will look better sooner

Definition appears quickly and it's motivating. That's the trap — cutting feels productive even when it isn't the thing you needed.

What it costs: you build very little muscle in a deficit, and long or aggressive cuts make it harder to hold what you already have.

The asymmetry matters. A cut done at the wrong time costs you months of building. A bulk done at the wrong time costs you some fat you'll have to lose later. Neither is fatal, but they aren't equally expensive.

When you don't have to choose

Body recomposition — building muscle and losing fat at once — is real, but not for everyone. It works well in three cases:

  • You're new to lifting. Beginners gain muscle rapidly enough that it happens even in a small deficit.
  • You're returning after a long break. Regaining previously-held muscle is much faster than building it the first time.
  • You're carrying a lot of fat and don't train. Plenty of stored energy, plenty of untapped adaptation.

Outside those three, recomp gets slow and frustrating. If you've trained consistently for a few years and you're reasonably lean, picking a direction beats trying to do both — you'll make more progress in six months of one than twelve months of neither.

How fast, once you've picked

Bulk0.25–0.5% of body weight per week — faster than this and the extra is mostly fat. Muscle simply can't be built at the speed people want it to be.
Cut0.5–1% of body weight per week — faster increases how much muscle goes with the fat, and makes the whole thing harder to stick to.

Questions

Should I bulk or cut first?
Use body fat percentage, not the mirror. For men, above roughly 20% body fat, cut first — a bulk from there adds fat faster than muscle and gets uncomfortable quickly. Below roughly 15%, bulk. Between 15 and 20% either works, so pick based on whether you want to look better sooner or build faster. For women the same logic applies with the thresholds shifted up by roughly 8 to 10 points.
Can I build muscle and lose fat at the same time?
Yes, but mainly in three situations: you're new to lifting, you're returning after a long break, or you're carrying a lot of fat while untrained. That's body recomposition. It works well in those cases and gets much slower once you're trained and reasonably lean — at which point picking a direction beats trying to do both.
How long should a bulk last?
Long enough to matter — usually several months at minimum, since muscle is gained slowly. A common approach is to bulk until body fat reaches the upper end of your comfortable range, then cut back down. Two or three week bulks accomplish very little beyond added water and food weight.
How fast should I gain or lose weight?
Roughly 0.25 to 0.5% of body weight per week for a bulk, and 0.5 to 1% per week for a cut. Faster bulking mostly adds fat, and faster cutting increases how much muscle you lose alongside it. General guidelines, not medical advice.
I'm lean but I don't look muscular. Bulk or cut?
Bulk. This is the single most common misread — being lean without visible muscle means there isn't enough muscle underneath, and cutting further only makes that more obvious. Leanness reveals muscle; it doesn't create it. A FFMI check will tell you quickly whether you're actually short on lean mass.
