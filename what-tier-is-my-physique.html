The quick self-check

Read from the bottom up and stop at the first one that's honestly true of you.

ENo visible training adaptation. Nothing in the mirror suggests you lift, regardless of how long you've been going. Everyone starts here.
DSomething is starting. Minimal but real change — maybe shoulders or arms — visible to you, probably not to anyone else.
CClearly training, clearly early. A genuine base exists and others would guess you lift, but there's obvious work left everywhere.
BSolid base, nameable gaps. You look built, and you can name two or three groups that are behind. Most consistent lifters live here for years.
AObviously built, in clothes. Large development across the whole body from years of work. Maybe one soft spot. More on A-tier.
SStands out among built people. Exceptional size, thickness and proportion, with nothing the eye catches on. Rare. More on S-tier.
If you're torn between two tiers, you're almost certainly the lower one — the boundary cases feel obvious from inside the higher tier and ambiguous from inside the lower one.

Why your own guess is probably wrong

If you train hard

You'll under-rate yourself

Your reference point isn't the general population — it's the most developed physiques you see online, which are a heavily filtered sample and frequently enhanced.

Measured against that, almost everyone looks average. Measured against people who actually exist, you're usually a tier higher than you think.

If you've plateaued

You'll over-rate yourself

You see yourself daily, so change — and the absence of change — is nearly invisible. Your mental image tends to lag behind reality, anchored on your best-lit day.

Pump, lighting and angle all inflate the read. Most people's honest tier is what they look like on a normal morning, not in the gym mirror mid-session.

Getting an outside read

Moving up

  • E through C — the answer is general. Train consistently, add load over time, eat enough to support growth. Nothing clever is required and nothing clever will speed it up.
  • B and above — the answer becomes specific. What holds you back is one or two lagging groups, not overall effort. Training everything harder mostly maintains the imbalance.
  • Proportion, not just size — shoulder width relative to waist does a lot of the visual work. Worth checking your shoulder-to-waist ratio and reading what makes a physique aesthetic.
  • Conditioning reveals it — if you're carrying enough fat to blur the outline, some of your apparent tier is hidden rather than missing.

What this isn't

Tiers are a grading convention, not a medical or scientific classification, and they describe visible muscular development only — not health, fitness, strength or worth. CutRank reads one photo and returns an AI estimate rather than a measurement; lighting, pose, pump and camera angle all move the result. Questions

What tier is my physique?
Tiers run S, A, B, C, D, E from elite down to untrained. Roughly: no visible training adaptation is E or D; a visible start with work left everywhere is C; a solid base with nameable weak points is B; large obvious development from years of training is A; and exceptional development that stands out even among well-built lifters is S. A photo scan gives you a graded answer free.
How accurate is judging your own physique?
Not very, and it fails in both directions. People who train hard tend to under-rate themselves because they compare against the most developed physiques online rather than the general population. People who've plateaued tend to over-rate, because gradual change is hard to perceive day to day.
What tier is the average gym goer?
Most people who train regularly but without structured progression sit around C or B — a visible base with several groups clearly behind. A-tier takes years of consistent progressive training, and S-tier is rare by design.
How do I move up a tier?
At lower tiers it's general: train consistently, progress the load, eat enough. From B upward it becomes specific, because what holds you back is usually one or two lagging groups rather than overall size. Knowing which group is behind matters more than training harder everywhere.
Do tiers account for body fat?
Indirectly. Tiers grade visible muscular development, and body fat affects how much of it is visible. A physique carrying more fat may grade lower than its underlying development warrants — which is why a body fat estimate alongside the tier is more useful than the letter alone.
